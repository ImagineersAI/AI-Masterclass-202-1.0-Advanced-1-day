How the day worksOne business tool, built in five steps.

You spend the day developing a complete business skill or plugin. Choose from 30 tools before the course, then build yours around three real examples from your work.

The skill or plugin is a folder of instructions, prompts, templates and a small program. You build and run it in Claude Cowork. Each step ends with a saved version you can return to.

A core skill you will learn

Work with Claude Cowork throughout the day.

We use Claude Cowork because it is well suited to building and running business tools. It works with your files, runs programs and handles multi-step tasks from a plain-English brief. You do not need to arrive knowing how to code.

Everyone uses the same platform so demonstrations, instructions and support are consistent. That keeps the day moving and gives you more time to build.

  • Work directly with your files. Have Cowork read source material and save prompts, templates and programs in your connected folder.
  • Run and check the work. Ask Cowork to run your program in its code environment, inspect the results and fix failures.

You practise this throughout all five steps, from the first prompt to the final batch tests and review. You leave knowing how to use Cowork to develop your next tool too.

  1. Step 1Write the prompt.Use the expert's method you learned on the first masterclass.Read why and what you will do

    Why this matters

    On the first masterclass, you learned to give the AI an expert's method, rather than only asking for an answer. Here you turn that method into instructions inside a reusable tool. You are building on a skill you already know.

    A useful business tool needs more than a request for a good answer. It needs the steps an experienced person would follow. If you ask for a recommendation before checking the facts, the AI may produce a convincing answer with no sound basis.

    The method changes with the job. A comparison needs the same facts from each supplier. A shortlist needs must-have requirements checked before scoring. A procedure needs its steps put in order before anyone writes the checklist.

    What you will do

    1. Define what goes in, who will use the result and what decision or task it must support.
    2. Write the method as a sequence: read the material, extract the relevant facts, apply the criteria, then produce the result.
    3. State what must be supported by evidence. Require a page, clause or quoted passage where appropriate. Tell the AI to flag missing facts and assumptions.
    4. Run the prompt twice on the same input and compare the answers. Save version one. Before coffee, start a trial on three real inputs.

    For example

    A board paper first extracts the facts, then states the decision sought, compares the options and writes the recommendation. An evidence brief collects source passages before making claims.

    What you will have

    A first prompt that describes a repeatable working method, plus saved outputs that show where it still makes mistakes.

  2. Step 2Have the AI interview you and improve it.Let the AI challenge your thinking and push for a tool you are proud of.Read why and what you will do

    Why this matters

    You know things about the job that your first prompt will leave unsaid. What counts as a serious change? Which requirement rules an option out? What must happen when information is missing?

    These answers determine how the tool should work. The interview also uncovers useful functions you may not have requested, such as showing what would change a decision. The AI asks questions; you decide the business rules.

    Ask the AI to stretch you. It should challenge vague answers, suggest functions you had not considered and push beyond your first idea. You learn to use it as a demanding design partner, working towards a tool you would be proud to put in your team's hands.

    What you will do

    1. Review the results from three real inputs. Ask the AI to explain its failures, challenge your assumptions and interview you about what would make the tool excellent.
    2. Define thresholds, weights, units, dates and terms. Specify the audience, required evidence and conditions that remove an option from consideration.
    3. Decide how the tool handles exceptions, ambiguity and missing material. Set the limits of its use and when it must ask for help.
    4. Add the useful functions the interview identifies. Rewrite the prompt, add a delivery checklist, rerun your examples and save version two.

    For example

    A management accounts tool asks whether a change must exceed a rand amount, a percentage or both. A decision tool asks which criteria overlap. A contract tool asks whether deadlines use calendar or business days.

    What you will have

    An improved prompt with explicit business rules, agreed functions, evidence requirements and clear instructions for cases it cannot answer.

  3. Step 3Create the template.Give your prompt, skill or plugin an app people can use.Read why and what you will do

    Why this matters

    A tool needs a consistent way to collect its inputs as well as a consistent result. If a colleague forgets the weights, start date or rate card, even a good method cannot produce the right answer.

    The result must also make the important information easy to find. A verdict, the reasons behind it and any missing evidence should appear in predictable places. The right format may be a table, chart, timeline, diagram or printable document.

    The input and result pages give your prompt, skill or plugin the interface of an app. Someone can supply the information and read the result without rebuilding your prompt. The pages provide the interface; your instructions and program provide the working method.

    What you will do

    1. Ask the AI to build an input page and a result page in HTML, the file format your browser displays.
    2. Give the input page clear fields for the documents, notes and settings your tool needs. Label required information and explain what each field means.
    3. Fix the result's headings, tables and places for evidence. Make missing information and warnings visible. Include print, download or interactive features where the chosen tool needs them.
    4. Tell the AI to fill these templates consistently. Save the version, deliberately damage it and practise restoring the earlier one.

    For example

    A supplier comparison collects the same facts for each quote and shows them side by side. A board paper puts the decision sought first. A case timeline keeps the source beside each event.

    What you will have

    Reusable input and result pages that make the tool easier to run, read and check, plus a saved version you can recover.

  4. Step 4Add the Python program.Make the result consistent and reduce the work you send to the AI.Read why and what you will do

    Why this matters

    A large language model (LLM) generates text from patterns learned during training. A convincing number does not prove a calculation was run. Models can solve maths and use calculators, but generated arithmetic can still be wrong. A board deck or budget needs figures that were calculated and checked.

    The same principle applies beyond money. Word limits, contract dates, duplicate records, scoring rules and exact quotes all need repeatable checks. Keep interpretation and drafting with the AI. Put precise calculations and rules into a program that follows the same instructions every run.

    That also reduces token use. Once written, the program can calculate, sort and check in Cowork’s code environment without another LLM call for each operation. The same input and rules give the same calculated result. You use the AI where judgement is needed and make the final product much more consistent.

    What you will do

    1. Identify everything your tool must calculate or check exactly. Ask the AI to write a small Python program for those jobs, replacing repeated model calls where possible.
    2. Make the program check the input and the result: required fields, valid dates, totals, limits and contradictions. Define when it must stop and name a problem.
    3. Have the AI use the program's results in its output. Check that figures in the commentary agree with the tables and that the evidence can be traced.
    4. Work out three results by hand and compare them with the program. Code can contain mistakes too, and a correct formula cannot repair a misread input.

    For example

    A funding application counts words and reconciles its budget. A timesheet catches overlapping hours. An evidence brief checks quotes against source text. A confidentiality checker applies the agreed verdict rules. Finding a quote verifies its wording, not whether its claim is true.

    What you will have

    A program that performs the exact work and reports failed checks, with results you have compared against known answers. Repeatable work runs as code, reducing unnecessary model calls and making the output more consistent.

  5. Step 5Test it and prepare it for handover.Have the AI create and run far more test cases than you would tackle by hand.Read why and what you will do

    Why this matters

    A polished page and one successful example do not establish that the tool works. It may fail on a missing value, a different file layout or a case just outside a rule. It must also handle a clean input without inventing problems.

    Asking an AI whether its work looks right is not enough. Each test needs an expected answer you can check independently. Another person must also be able to use the tool without relying on instructions that exist only in your head.

    You do not have to invent and run every case yourself. Teach the AI to generate synthetic test inputs, vary them systematically and run them in large batches. It can explore combinations you would rarely have time to try by hand. You check how the expected answers were established and review the failures.

    What you will do

    1. Ask the AI to generate ordinary cases, difficult cases and deliberately broken inputs. Set rules for the expected answers and verify a sample independently.
    2. Include missing information, contradictory figures, duplicates, ties, different layouts and values exactly at a rule's limit, as relevant to your tool.
    3. Have the AI write and run a batch test program. Compare actual and expected results, report failures and check source evidence. Confirm that unsupported inputs trigger a warning or stop. Save the cases and rerun the batch after fixes.
    4. Start a separate Cowork review task to look for faults. Add start-here instructions and sample inputs, then let a neighbour run the tool without help. Fix what fails and save the finished version to GitHub.

    For example

    A contract test includes a leap day and a weekend deadline. A shortlist test includes a strong candidate missing a must-have. A quote check includes one altered word. A clean roster must report no rule breaks.

    What you will have

    A tool with a repeatable batch of tests, checked examples, clear limits and instructions another person has tried. At 15:30, share it with the group using sample inputs.

What every tool on this list has in common

  • It brings a skill you may not have, or saves hours. Each one carries an expert's method inside it.
  • It needs very little to start. One document, a few files or a page of your own notes is enough.
  • Something in it has to be exactly right. A small program does the sums, dates and checks, so the AI cannot get them wrong.

Board and big decisions

1. Board paper writer.

A manager's rough notes and figures go in. A board paper comes out in your format: the decision sought, the options scored, the money table and the risks. The program adds up the money table and refuses to deliver if a figure in the text differs from the table.

2. Decision table with a flip test.

Your options, criteria and weights go in. A ranked table comes out. The program then tests every weight and reports the smallest change that would change the winner, which shows how safe the decision is.

3. Negotiation prep sheet.

A page of notes on the deal goes in. One page comes out with both sides' interests, your walk-away point and the range where a deal is possible. It also plans what to concede and in what order. The program values each concession in rands.

Money

4. Management accounts commentary.

One month's management accounts go in. One page comes out that explains every change above a set size against budget and last year, with a chart of what moved profit. The program works out every change, so the words cannot disagree with the numbers. This is the safe choice for anyone.

5. Investor update with runway.

A short table of the month's key figures and your notes go in. An investor update comes out in the standard order: highlights, problems, figures and requests. The program works out the monthly changes and how many months of cash are left.

6. Annual accounts red-flag reader.

One set of annual financial statements goes in. One page comes out with the key ratios and two warning scores that analysts use (Altman Z and Beneish M). Every flag carries the page number behind it. A board member or lender gets an analyst's first read in minutes.

7. Timesheet and bill from your week.

Your calendar export and a list of sent emails go in. A draft timesheet comes out by client, then invoice lines at the right rate for each client. The program rounds time to your billing units and applies the rates.

8. Fee and scope page.

Notes from a scoping call and your rate card go in. The scope, phases, hours, fees and payment dates come out. This is the manageable part of a proposal, and the part where mistakes cost money.

Deals and funding

9. First-look deal screen.

A teaser or information memorandum goes in. One page comes out: fit against your mandate, any automatic rejections, a rough returns sum and the ten questions to ask first.

10. Term sheet comparison with exit payouts.

Two term sheets go in. Every difference comes out side by side. The program works out who gets paid what at several sale prices, which is where the terms really differ.

11. Data room check.

The list of files in a data room and your due diligence checklist go in. One page comes out showing what is present, what is missing and the percentage complete by area, with a request list ready to send.

12. Impact report.

A small table of project results and targets goes in. A funder-ready page comes out: results against targets, cost per person reached and a diagram linking money to outcomes.

13. Funding application builder.

The funder's questions and your project notes go in. A drafted application comes out. The program holds every answer inside its word limit, checks that no question is skipped and makes the budget add up.

Bids and buying

14. Bid evaluation sheet.

The bids and the published criteria go in. A score for each bidder comes out with reasons. The program applies the 80/20 or 90/10 price formula and ranks the bidders.

15. Bid CVs in one format.

Staff CVs in any layout go in. CVs come out in the format the tender demands. The program works out years of experience from the dates and keeps each CV inside the page limit.

16. Request for quotation drafter.

A page of notes on what you need goes in. A request for quotation comes out with a clear specification, a reply form and the scoring sheet you will use on the replies.

17. Supplier quote comparison.

Three quotes go in. One page comes out comparing them on the same basis: tax, delivery, payment terms and warranty. The program works out the true cost over the life of the purchase.

People

18. CV shortlister.

A job description and a handful of CVs go in. A ranked shortlist comes out, with the evidence from each CV behind every score. The program applies your weights and removes anyone who fails a must-have.

19. Interview pack and panel score.

A job description and one CV go in. Questions aimed at the gaps in that CV come out, with a scoring sheet. After the interview, the program combines the panel's scores and shows where the panel disagreed.

20. Roster checker.

One week's or month's roster goes in. A list of rule breaks comes out, such as too little rest between shifts or too many nights in a row. The program also shows who carries more than a fair share of weekends and nights.

21. Organisation chart reader.

A list of names, job titles and managers goes in. A chart you can click through comes out. The program counts the layers and how many people report to each manager, and flags managers with too many or too few.

22. Procedure from a voice note.

Record yourself explaining a job for ten minutes. A written procedure comes out with numbered steps, a checklist, who is responsible for each step and a short quiz for staff. The program checks that every step has exactly one owner.

Operations and projects

23. Process map with the bottleneck.

A description of a process and rough times for each step go in. A diagram comes out showing who does what. The program finds the step that limits the whole process and the share of time that adds value.

24. Project money check.

A short task list with budget, spend and percentage complete goes in. One page comes out saying whether the project is ahead or behind for the money spent, and what it will cost at the finish. It uses the standard method that project managers call earned value.

25. WhatsApp group to action list.

One exported group chat goes in. One page comes out with the decisions made, the actions by person and the questions nobody answered. The program reads the dates and counts how long each request waited.

Documents

26. Contract dates and duties.

One contract goes in. Every duty and deadline comes out on one page and as a calendar file you can import. The program works out the real dates, such as the last day to give notice.

27. Confidentiality agreement triage.

One confidentiality agreement and your standard terms go in. A verdict comes out: sign, negotiate or refer to a lawyer, with the clauses behind it. This is the simplest tool on the list to build.

28. Case timeline.

A bundle of letters, emails and notes goes in. A dated timeline comes out with a source for every entry. The program sorts the dates, measures the gaps and flags entries that contradict each other.

29. Evidence brief with checked quotes.

Five to ten reports go in. A briefing note comes out with a quote and page number for each claim. The program checks that every quote appears word for word in its source, and refuses to deliver if one does not.

30. Claims checker.

One draft article or report goes in. A list comes out of every claim that can be checked and the source it needs. The program recalculates every percentage and total in the text.

Check your computer skills before bookingBefore the day: readiness and setup.

Check that this course is right for you

You do not need coding experience. You do need to use your own laptop confidently and complete everyday computer tasks without step-by-step help. The day moves quickly and assumes these skills.

Before booking, check that you can do the following independently:

  • Select text, copy, cut and paste using Command/Ctrl+C/X/V on Mac/Windows
  • Switch between open apps and browser tabs, then return to the task you were working on.
  • Open an installed app from the Start menu or Applications folder. Recognise the difference between the app and its downloaded installer.
  • Use File Explorer on Windows or Finder on Mac to find Downloads and navigate to a working folder.
  • Download a file, save it in a chosen folder and find it again, rather than relying on the browser's preview.
  • Create and rename folders, move or copy files between them, and recognise which copy of a file you are opening.
  • Extract a ZIP file and work with the files in the extracted folder.
  • Upload or attach the correct file by choosing it from a folder.
  • Sign in to the correct online account and complete an email or phone verification when asked.
  • Follow numbered written instructions, recognise when a task has finished or shown an error, and take a screenshot to ask for help.

If these tasks still need regular help, build that confidence before booking this advanced course. Practise with someone who can help you, then return to this checklist. We can help with the course tools and setup; the day does not include teaching everyday computer use.
