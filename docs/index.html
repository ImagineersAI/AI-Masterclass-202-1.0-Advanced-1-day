How the day works

One business tool, built in five steps.

You spend the day developing a complete business tool. Choose from 30 tools before the course, then build yours around three real examples from your work.

Use Claude Cowork to work with your files, run programs and save a version you can return to at each step.

  1. Step 1Write the prompt.Use the expert's method you learned on the first masterclass.Read why and what you will have

    Why this matters

    Your first masterclass taught you to give the AI an expert’s method. Here you turn that method into reusable instructions for a real job. The prompt must check the facts and apply your criteria before it recommends an answer.

    What you will do

    Define the inputs, write the method and try your prompt on three real examples.

    What you will have

    A first prompt that describes a repeatable working method, plus saved outputs that show where it still makes mistakes.

  2. Step 2Have the AI interview you and improve it.Let the AI challenge your thinking and push for a tool you are proud of.Read why and what you will have

    Why this matters

    Your first prompt will leave some of your knowledge unsaid. The AI interviews you about missing facts, exceptions and business rules. It challenges vague answers and suggests useful functions; you decide what the tool must do.

    What you will do

    Use your trial results and the AI’s questions to agree the rules and improve the prompt.

    What you will have

    An improved prompt with explicit business rules, agreed functions, evidence requirements and clear instructions for cases it cannot answer.

  3. Step 3Create the template.Give your tool input and result pages people can use.Read why and what you will have

    Why this matters

    Consistent input and result pages make your tool easier for someone else to use. Required fields prevent missing information. A predictable layout keeps the decision, reasons, evidence and warnings easy to find.

    What you will do

    Build reusable input and result pages, then practise recovering a saved version.

    What you will have

    Reusable input and result pages that make the tool easier to run, read and check, plus a saved version you can recover.

  4. Step 4Add the Python program.Use Python for calculations and checks that need to give the same result every time.Read why and what you will have

    Why this matters

    Generated arithmetic can be wrong even when it looks convincing. Totals, dates, scoring rules, word limits and exact quotes need repeatable calculations and checks. Python runs those rules consistently and reduces repeated AI calls. Code can contain mistakes too, so you check it against known answers.

    What you will do

    Have the AI write the program and compare its results with three answers you have checked independently.

    What you will have

    A program that performs calculations and exact checks, reports failures and uses results you have compared against known answers.

  5. Step 5Test it and prepare it for handover.Have the AI create and run far more test cases than you would tackle by hand.Read why and what you will have

    Why this matters

    One successful example does not show that a tool works reliably. Asking an AI whether its work looks right is not enough. Tests need expected answers you can check independently. Another person must also be able to use the tool without your help.

    What you will do

    Run batches of normal and difficult cases, review failures and let a neighbour try the tool.

    What you will have

    A tested tool with checked examples, clear limits and instructions another person has tried. Share it with the group using sample inputs.

What every tool on this list has in common

  • It brings a skill you may not have, or saves hours. Each one carries an expert's method inside it.
  • It needs very little to start. One document, a few files or a page of your own notes is enough.
  • Something in it has to be exactly right. A small program does the sums, dates and checks, so the AI cannot get them wrong.

You already take home our finished tools, so they are not on this list. They are the Excel model builder, the board pack reader, the dashboard builder, the Gantt creator, the hand-drawn slide, the slide script builders, the explainer video and the issue tree.

Board and big decisions

1. Board paper writer.

A manager's rough notes and figures go in. A board paper comes out in your format: the decision sought, the options scored, the money table and the risks. The program adds up the money table and refuses to deliver if a figure in the text differs from the table.

2. Decision table with a flip test.

Your options, criteria and weights go in. A ranked table comes out. The program then tests every weight and reports the smallest change that would change the winner, which shows how safe the decision is.

3. Negotiation prep sheet.

A page of notes on the deal goes in. One page comes out with both sides' interests, your walk-away point and the range where a deal is possible. It also plans what to concede and in what order. The program values each concession in rands.

Money

4. Management accounts commentary.

One month's management accounts go in. One page comes out that explains every change above a set size against budget and last year, with a chart of what moved profit. The program works out every change, so the words cannot disagree with the numbers. This is the safe choice for anyone.

5. Investor update with runway.

A short table of the month's key figures and your notes go in. An investor update comes out in the standard order: highlights, problems, figures and requests. The program works out the monthly changes and how many months of cash are left.

6. Annual accounts red-flag reader.

One set of annual financial statements goes in. One page comes out with the key ratios and two warning scores that analysts use (Altman Z and Beneish M). Every flag carries the page number behind it. A board member or lender gets an analyst's first read in minutes.

7. Timesheet and bill from your week.

Your calendar export and a list of sent emails go in. A draft timesheet comes out by client, then invoice lines at the right rate for each client. The program rounds time to your billing units and applies the rates.

8. Fee and scope page.

Notes from a scoping call and your rate card go in. The scope, phases, hours, fees and payment dates come out. This is the manageable part of a proposal, and the part where mistakes cost money.

Deals and funding

9. First-look deal screen.

A teaser or information memorandum goes in. One page comes out: fit against your mandate, any automatic rejections, a rough returns sum and the ten questions to ask first.

10. Term sheet comparison with exit payouts.

Two term sheets go in. Every difference comes out side by side. The program works out who gets paid what at several sale prices, which is where the terms really differ.

11. Data room check.

The list of files in a data room and your due diligence checklist go in. One page comes out showing what is present, what is missing and the percentage complete by area, with a request list ready to send.

12. Impact report.

A small table of project results and targets goes in. A funder-ready page comes out: results against targets, cost per person reached and a diagram linking money to outcomes.

13. Funding application builder.

The funder's questions and your project notes go in. A drafted application comes out. The program holds every answer inside its word limit, checks that no question is skipped and makes the budget add up.

Bids and buying

14. Bid evaluation sheet.

The bids and the published criteria go in. A score for each bidder comes out with reasons. The program applies the 80/20 or 90/10 price formula and ranks the bidders.

15. Bid CVs in one format.

Staff CVs in any layout go in. CVs come out in the format the tender demands. The program works out years of experience from the dates and keeps each CV inside the page limit.

16. Request for quotation drafter.

A page of notes on what you need goes in. A request for quotation comes out with a clear specification, a reply form and the scoring sheet you will use on the replies.

17. Supplier quote comparison.

Three quotes go in. One page comes out comparing them on the same basis: tax, delivery, payment terms and warranty. The program works out the true cost over the life of the purchase.

People

18. CV shortlister.

A job description and a handful of CVs go in. A ranked shortlist comes out, with the evidence from each CV behind every score. The program applies your weights and removes anyone who fails a must-have.

19. Interview pack and panel score.

A job description and one CV go in. Questions aimed at the gaps in that CV come out, with a scoring sheet. After the interview, the program combines the panel's scores and shows where the panel disagreed.

20. Roster checker.

One week's or month's roster goes in. A list of rule breaks comes out, such as too little rest between shifts or too many nights in a row. The program also shows who carries more than a fair share of weekends and nights.

21. Organisation chart reader.

A list of names, job titles and managers goes in. A chart you can click through comes out. The program counts the layers and how many people report to each manager, and flags managers with too many or too few.

22. Procedure from a voice note.

Record yourself explaining a job for ten minutes. A written procedure comes out with numbered steps, a checklist, who is responsible for each step and a short quiz for staff. The program checks that every step has exactly one owner.

Operations and projects

23. Process map with the bottleneck.

A description of a process and rough times for each step go in. A diagram comes out showing who does what. The program finds the step that limits the whole process and the share of time that adds value.

24. Project money check.

A short task list with budget, spend and percentage complete goes in. One page comes out saying whether the project is ahead or behind for the money spent, and what it will cost at the finish. It uses the standard method that project managers call earned value.

25. WhatsApp group to action list.

One exported group chat goes in. One page comes out with the decisions made, the actions by person and the questions nobody answered. The program reads the dates and counts how long each request waited.

Documents

26. Contract dates and duties.

One contract goes in. Every duty and deadline comes out on one page and as a calendar file you can import. The program works out the real dates, such as the last day to give notice.

27. Confidentiality agreement triage.

One confidentiality agreement and your standard terms go in. A verdict comes out: sign, negotiate or refer to a lawyer, with the clauses behind it. This is the simplest tool on the list to build.

28. Case timeline.

A bundle of letters, emails and notes goes in. A dated timeline comes out with a source for every entry. The program sorts the dates, measures the gaps and flags entries that contradict each other.

29. Evidence brief with checked quotes.

Five to ten reports go in. A briefing note comes out with a quote and page number for each claim. The program checks that every quote appears word for word in its source, and refuses to deliver if one does not.

30. Claims checker.

One draft article or report goes in. A list comes out of every claim that can be checked and the source it needs. The program recalculates every percentage and total in the text.

Check your computer skills before bookingBefore the day: readiness and setup.

Check that this course is right for you

You do not need coding experience. You do need to use your own laptop confidently and complete everyday computer tasks without step-by-step help. The day moves quickly and assumes these skills.

Before booking, check that you can do the following independently:

  • Select text, copy, cut and paste using Command/Ctrl+C/X/V on Mac/Windows
  • Switch between open apps and browser tabs, then return to the task you were working on.
  • Open an installed app from the Start menu or Applications folder. Recognise the difference between the app and its downloaded installer.
  • Use File Explorer on Windows or Finder on Mac to find Downloads and navigate to a working folder.
  • Download a file, save it in a chosen folder and find it again, rather than relying on the browser's preview.
  • Create and rename folders, move or copy files between them, and recognise which copy of a file you are opening.
  • Extract a ZIP file and work with the files in the extracted folder.
  • Upload or attach the correct file by choosing it from a folder.
  • Sign in to the correct online account and complete an email or phone verification when asked.
  • Follow numbered written instructions, recognise when a task has finished or shown an error, and take a screenshot to ask for help.

If these tasks still need regular help, build that confidence before booking this advanced course. Practise with someone who can help you, then return to this checklist. We can help with the course tools and setup; the day does not include teaching everyday computer use.
